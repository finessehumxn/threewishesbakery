Feeding your starter, like a person you love.

Patti's honest, forgiving routine for a sourdough starter that actually shows up.

Nurse Patti5 min readThree Wishes Bakery ✦ California

An open crumb like this comes from a starter that was fed, not one that was rescued.

Ratio
1 : 5 : 5
Peak
4–8 hrs
Between bakes
Fridge, weekly

I work twelve-hour shifts. I am not getting up at 4am to feed a jar of flour on a schedule, and neither are you. Here is the routine I actually keep, which is the only kind of routine worth writing down.

The ratio, and why it isn't 1:1:1

Most beginner instructions say equal parts starter, flour, and water by weight. That works, but it means your starter peaks in about three hours and then spends the rest of the day hungry and going sour.

I feed 1 part starter to 5 parts flour to 5 parts water — say 20g of starter, 100g flour, 100g water. Fewer yeast cells and more food means a slower, longer rise. It peaks in six to eight hours instead of three, which means I can feed it before a shift and use it after one.

A smaller feed isn't weaker. It's just on a schedule a working person can keep.

Reading the jar

Forget the clock and watch the starter. Put a rubber band around the jar at the level of the fresh feed, and learn these three states:

  • Rising. Doming on top, bubbles through the sides, climbing past the band. It's working.
  • Peak. Roughly doubled, domed, and smelling like yogurt and something faintly sweet. This is the hour to use it.
  • Falling. The dome has sunk into a crater and the smell has gone sharp, like nail polish or vinegar. It's still alive, just hungry. Feed it and wait; don't bake with it.

The float test lies sometimes

You've read that a spoonful of ready starter floats in water. It usually does. But a stiff starter can sink at perfect peak, and a slack one can float while it's already falling. Use the float test as a second opinion, never as the verdict. The dome and the smell are the truth.

Between bakes: the fridge

I bake bread once a month. My starter does not live on the counter for the other twenty-nine days.

  1. Feed it, let it rise about a third of the way, then lid it and put it in the fridge.
  2. Feed it once a week if you remember. Every two weeks is survivable. It gets sluggish, not dead.
  3. Three days before you want to bake, bring it out and start feeding it once a day at room temperature.
  4. By the third feed it should be doubling predictably. That's when it's ready to make bread, not before.

Things that look like disaster and aren't

  • A layer of dark liquid on top. That's hooch — alcohol from a starter that ran out of food. Pour it off or stir it in, then feed. Harmless.
  • A sharp acetone smell. Same message: hungry. Feed it twice in a day and it comes back.
  • No activity for a day after the fridge. Normal. Cold starters take two or three feeds to wake up.

When to actually throw it out

Fuzzy mold — pink, orange, black, or anything raised and dry-looking on the surface — means start over. Not worth the risk, not worth the argument. Discard is cheap; a bad bake isn't.

A starter is a low-maintenance dependent, not a high-maintenance one. Feed it when you can, keep it cold when you can't, and it will forgive you the rest.
